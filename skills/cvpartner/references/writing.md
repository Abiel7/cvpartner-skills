# Writing CV text

The reader is a manager or a customer deciding whether to put this consultant on their project. They skim. Every text answers three questions: what did the customer need, what was delivered, and what difference it made.

The examples below are invented to show the pattern. Their numbers are illustrative; in a real CV use only numbers the user or evidence confirms.

## House style and the user's voice

Before writing, find the company's CV convention: ask the user, or ask them to open a colleague's CV as an example. Many consultancies write CVs in the third person with the consultant's first name ("Kari er…", "Kari is…"), and that convention wins over this guide. Then:

- Keep one person and one tense across the whole CV. Bullets without a subject ("Designed…", "Led…") fit a third-person CV.
- Use the consultant's name rather than a pronoun unless the user tells you which pronoun to use.
- Keep words the user already reaches for in their CV and notes, and avoid introducing words they never use. A CV that sounds like its owner survives the interview.

## Accuracy first

- **Every claim has a source**: the user's own account, the code, or their mail and chat. A fact without one is a question for the user, never a guess.
- **Reframe emphasis, never substance.** Leading with the most relevant work, or naming it in the reader's terms, is fine. Claiming work, scope or results the consultant did not have is not.
- **Apply the interview test** to every line: could the consultant explain it in an interview without backtracking ("well, what I actually meant was…")? A line that fails is a _stretch_. Show it to the user with the reason and ask: keep, soften or drop?
- **Style never changes facts.** Keep a real number even when it breaks the rhythm; never add detail to make a line sound better.

## Profile summary

The profile tells the reader what kind of consultant this is and what they work with. Customers and projects already have their own section, so the profile names none of them.

Two or three sentences, in the house style:

1. **Who.** Role and seniority, and how the consultant works with customers.
2. **What.** The areas of experience, broadest first: platforms, kinds of solutions, how the work has developed.
3. **How.** Two or three working traits the CV backs up, such as learning new domains quickly or explaining technical choices plainly.

> **Before:** Passionate, proactive and results-driven developer with a proven track record of delivering robust, innovative solutions for leading companies in fast-paced environments.
>
> **After:** Kari is a senior developer and architect who works closely with customers and takes ownership of a solution from the first idea until it runs in production. Broad experience with integration and data platforms in the cloud, from reporting to automation. Quick to learn new business domains and explains technical choices in plain terms.

Only use a trait the CV supports: "works closely with customers" needs projects where the consultant did, "shares knowledge" needs mentoring, talks or advising. Personality words a reader cannot check (proactive, dynamic, energetic, passionate) say nothing.

CV Partner can hold several profile texts, each with a label. A consultant who bids for different kinds of roles can keep one per kind; see [tailoring.md](tailoring.md).

## Project description

Two or three sentences:

1. **Need.** The customer's problem, naming the people who had it ("dispatchers", "case workers", "nurses") rather than "users".
2. **Delivery.** What the project delivered, in business terms.
3. **Value.** What changed: time saved, errors removed, cost avoided, people reached.

> **Before:** Built a .NET API with Azure Functions and Cosmos DB and a React front end, deployed with Bicep. Currently in pilot.
>
> **After:** The customer's dispatchers planned about 400 deliveries a day by hand in spreadsheets. The project delivered a planning tool that proposes routes and lets dispatchers adjust them on a map. Daily planning now takes minutes instead of hours.

## Role description

A first line with the role, the team and the scope, then bullets. Each bullet follows **action → what → result**: a past-tense verb, what the consultant built, led or decided, and why it mattered.

> **Before:** I was responsible for the backend and did a lot of the integration work with Logic Apps and Service Bus, roughly 60 % of my time.
>
> **After:** Lead developer in a team of four.
> - Designed the link between the case system and the document archive, which ended manual re-filing of documents.
> - Set up automated testing and releases, so the team could ship every week instead of every quarter.
> - Coached two junior developers through their first production release.

First lines that work: "Technical lead for a team of six." / "Only developer, from first prototype to production." / "Architect in a joint team with the customer's IT department."

- **Budget by relevance and age.** Recent or most relevant projects get three to four bullets, older ones two or three, the oldest one or two.
- **Vary the verbs.** Use a different opening verb in each bullet of a role: _designed, built, led, cut, moved, set up, coached, replaced_.
- **Match the verb to the ownership.** Use _led, designed, built, decided_ for the consultant's own work. Keep _helped, contributed to, supported_ for work someone else owned, and use them only then: "helped", "assisted", "worked on" and "responsible for" undersell real ownership.
- **Prefer specifics.** "Cut the monthly close from eight days to three" beats "improved efficiency".

## Long engagements

Compare each project's length with how much work its text shows. Two years described by one bullet reads as low output, whether or not that is fair. Fix it honestly:

1. **Surface more real work.** Ask the user what else the period held: other deliveries, tooling, support, onboarding of colleagues.
2. **Show the phases.** "Started in support, then took over the integration platform" reads as growth; one undivided block reads as standing still.
3. **Name what made it long.** Regulatory approval, validation with domain experts, a staged rollout.

Never pad with invented work, and never shorten the dates.

## Customer description

One or two sentences about the customer itself, never the project.

> **Before:** The hospital wanted a new booking system.
>
> **After:** A regional hospital trust serving about 300,000 people across four hospitals.

## Words and habits to leave out

Technologies go in the project's skills list, so the text describes the work and its value. Leave out:

- **Implementation detail:** frameworks, cloud services, database names, architecture patterns.
- **Status updates:** "in pilot", "on its way to production", "phase 2 starts soon".
- **Time accounting:** "about a month, on and off", "60 % of my time".
- **First person:** "I", "me", "my", and the local equivalents such as _jeg_, _meg_, _jag_.
- **Clichés and filler,** which a reader skips and which mark text as generated: _passionate about, results-driven, proven track record, leverage, utilize, spearheaded, orchestrated, championed, synergy, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, innovative, best practices, pivotal, significant impact, fast-paced, team player, go-getter_. Replace each with the plain verb or the specific fact behind it.
- **Humble or apologetic wording:** "tried to", "was involved in", "some experience with". State what was done.
- **Long dashes (— and –) inside sentences.** Use a comma, a full stop or a new sentence. Date ranges such as "2023–2024" are fine.

## English and the local language

Write both versions with the same facts, numbers and dates. The local text is natural, idiomatic language for a local reader, never a word-for-word translation:

- Use the term the customer's industry uses in that language. Ask the user when unsure.
- Keep product and technology names in English: Microsoft 365, SharePoint, Azure, Salesforce.
- Follow the language's own capitalisation, number and date conventions.

### Norwegian (bokmål)

| English | Norwegian |
|---|---|
| Developer / Senior Developer | Utvikler / Senior utvikler |
| Software Developer | Systemutvikler |
| Lead Developer | Hovedutvikler |
| Architect / Lead Architect | Arkitekt / Ledende arkitekt |
| Technical Lead | Teknisk leder |
| Project Manager | Prosjektleder |
| Intern | Praktikant |
| case workers | saksbehandlere |
| service desk | brukerstøtte |
| go-live | produksjonssetting |
| rollout | utrulling |
| stakeholders | interessenter |

- Capitalise only the first word of a title: _Arkitekt og utvikler_, not "Arkitekt og Utvikler".
- Numbers use a space for thousands and a comma for decimals: _18 000_, _2,5 millioner_, _90 %_.
- Prefer Norwegian verbs to anglicisms: _støtte_, not "supporte"; _rulle ut_ or _ta i bruk_, not "deploye".

The project example above in Norwegian:

> Kundens transportledere planla rundt 400 leveranser om dagen manuelt i regneark. Prosjektet leverte et planleggingsverktøy som foreslår ruter og lar transportlederne justere dem i et kart. Den daglige planleggingen tar nå minutter i stedet for timer.

Add a section like this one for each local language your team uses.

## Review checklist

Before showing drafts to the user, run `cvp.lint()` on them for the mechanical checks, then read them once as the customer's hiring manager would, ideally in a fresh context that has only the drafts and `cv-work/facts.md`. Done when every item holds for every draft:

- Every claim, number and date traces to a confirmed line in the facts file; stretches are flagged with their reason.
- The text follows the house style and keeps one person and tense throughout.
- `cvp.lint()` reports nothing, or only lines you have checked and can explain (a "helped" that is true, for example).
- The profile names role, areas and backed-up traits, and no customers or projects.
- Each project text gives need, delivery and value; each role text opens with role and scope.
- No implementation detail in project or role text; technologies sit in the skills list.
- The verbs match the ownership the facts file records.
- Long engagements show enough work, or their phases.
- Dates, titles and numbers agree across the profile, work experience and projects, and between the two languages.
